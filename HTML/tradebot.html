TRADE BOT

~24,000 lines. Five signals. No edge.

A robot built to trade stocks automatically, and to prove first whether it actually had an edge before risking real money. It watched prices, news, company fundamentals, the economy, and even Reddit, blended all of that into a single buy/sell decision, and wrapped the whole thing in safety brakes. Then it spent weeks pointing that machinery at itself. Every measurement came back the same way: no edge. It never placed a single real trade.

The rule it had to follow

[ NO PROOF, NO TRADE ]

Before any code was written, the goal was written down in plain language, and it wasn't "make money." It was: prove there's a real advantage first, and if you can't prove it, don't trade. That single rule is why this project ends the way it does. The bot worked fine, technically. It just failed the test it was built to run on itself.

config.yaml YAML 
# the actual goal, word for word, before a line of code existed
capital_usd: 10000
min_sharpe: 1.0
max_drawdown: 0.15
success_criteria: "Beat SPY buy-and-hold, out-of-sample, net of fees,
                   over >= 60 trading days, Sharpe >= min_sharpe"
universe: ["AAPL","MSFT","NVDA","GOOGL","AMZN","META","TSLA","SPY"]
mode: "paper"   # NEVER default to live

In plain English: it had to beat a lazy "just buy an S&P 500 index fund and hold it" strategy, on money it had never seen before, after paying realistic trading fees, or it wasn't allowed to touch real money at all. The build brief itself even warned against the exact trap the project fell into: "A sophisticated pipeline wrapped around a signal with no real edge is still a coin flip with extra steps."

How it decided what to do

[ LOOK → SCORE → BLEND → DECIDE → SAFETY-CHECK ]

Every trading day, for 8 big-name stocks, the bot ran the same five-step pipeline. None of this is theoretical. All of it ran against real market history.

01
LOOK AROUND Pull five years of prices, news articles, company financials, economic data, and Reddit chatter. Five separate data sources, cross-checked against each other so a bad feed can't quietly poison a decision.
02
GET FIVE OPINIONS Five independent "signals" each rate a stock from sell to buy: chart patterns, news sentiment (read by an AI model), company fundamentals, the overall economy, and Reddit hype. Each one also reports how confident it is. The Reddit-hype signal, for example, is built to shut up entirely rather than guess when its AI filter is down.
03
BLEND THE OPINIONS The five opinions are combined into one number, weighted by how much each one is trusted. More trusted signals get more say. As you'll see below, that trust was badly misplaced.
04
DECIDE Only acts when the expected payoff clearly clears its own uncertainty. Otherwise it does nothing. "Not sure" always resolves to "don't trade." This is the rule from the config file, enforced in code.
05
SAFETY-CHECK A last set of hard rules can still veto the trade: is an earnings report about to drop, is there enough trading volume, do the fees eat the expected profit, has it lost too much today? These limits can't be talked around by anything upstream, including the bot's own self-improvement code (more on that below).

The brakes actually worked

[ 3,473 : 359 : 635 ]

Before ever letting it run, a three-year dry run tested whether the "don't trade unless you're sure" rule actually held up in practice. It did. The bot said "no" far more than it said "yes."

3,473 times It wasn't confident enough, so it did nothing
359 times It wanted to trade, but a safety rule vetoed it
635 times It actually traded (all on paper, with pretend money)

Restart-safe If the program crashed and restarted mid-trade, it was built to never accidentally place the same order twice, verified live on the paper account

The autopsy

[ FOUR QUESTIONS, FOUR "NO"S ]

Here's the important part. Once the machinery was built, it was pointed at itself to answer one question: does any of this actually predict anything? Four separate investigations, each one measured, not guessed at. Every one came back the same way.

the main opinion

The signal in charge of 60% of every decision was just guessing

The chart-pattern signal (the one given the most say in every same-day decision) was checked against what stocks actually did next. It called the right direction about as often as a coin flip, and for next-day moves it was wrong more often than right on 6 of the 8 stocks. It had been given the most trust specifically because it was "the only one we'd ever checked," which turned out to be exactly backwards.

the news reader

Reading the news didn't help either

30% of every decision leaned on an AI reading news headlines about the stock. Once it was actually possible to test this properly against history, the news signal also showed no real ability to predict direction. If anything, it leaned slightly the wrong way. Combined with the finding above, roughly 90% of the daily decision was resting on two signals that carried no real information.

the one hopeful lead

The one promising sign turned out to be two lucky quarters

One hint looked real: how strongly the bot felt (not which direction) seemed to predict good trades. Tested properly across three years split into 15 three-month chunks, it worked in only 8 of 15, a coin flip, and the entire apparent effect came from just two unusually good quarters. Told explicitly to "report honestly if it dies, don't soften that," the investigation did exactly that.

the final check

What looked like skill was actually just riding the market up

Last theory: maybe the bot was quietly riding a well-known market trend, buying stocks that are already going up. That isn't skill. It's something anyone can buy cheaply through an index fund. Checked directly: that trend was worth about +9.6% a year during the test window, and the bot's trades captured a grand total of +0.09% of it. It wasn't even good at accidentally free-riding a trend.

Three good months wearing a trench coat

[ WHERE THE "PROFIT" ACTUALLY CAME FROM ]

The backtest covered three years, split into 15 three-month stretches. Take away the best few, and the story falls apart.

Put another way: three good quarters made up 76% of the entire three-year "win." The other twelve quarters (three full years of trading) added up to essentially nothing (+1.9% combined). A strategy that only works because of a few lucky months isn't a strategy you can trust going forward.

Near-misses worth remembering

[ CAUGHT BEFORE THEY SHIPPED ]

Not everything here is bad news about trading. A lot of it is just good, careful engineering. These are specific bugs that were caught before they could quietly wreck a result.

The almost-fake discovery A measuring mistake made a crypto signal look like a strong, real prediction. Fixing the mistake made the "discovery" disappear. Caught before anyone got excited about it.
The rule that would've blocked everything A "don't trade if there's too little volume" safety rule, applied with the wrong numbers, would have blocked trading Bitcoin and Ethereum entirely, the two most heavily traded cryptocurrencies that exist.
The "crisis" alarm that never stopped ringing A setting that correctly flags "this is a crazy, dangerous trading day" for stocks fired on 0% of days for the S&P 500, but on 70% of days for Ethereum, since crypto is naturally more volatile. Had to be recalibrated per asset.
A math bug worth $1.7 quadrillion a day A unit mismatch in how crypto trading volume was calculated would have quietly reported Bitcoin trading $1.7 quadrillion a day. Caught before it could poison a backtest.
The trading fee that was set to zero Stock trades are commission-free, so that assumption got copy-pasted into the crypto code, where trading actually costs real fees. Left unfixed, it would have made crypto trading look artificially profitable.
Confidence that was fake-narrow The bot's "how sure am I" measurement for crypto came out far too confident, nearly 5 times tighter than it should have been, simply because it was tracking fewer things. Left deliberately un-patched rather than quietly fudged with a guessed number.

What it's built on

Everything here is real, working software: a broker connection for pretend ("paper") trading, five separate data feeds, an AI model for reading news, a local AI chatbot for filtering out Reddit noise, and a dashboard to watch it all happen. What it never did

[ THE NUMBER THAT ENDS THE STORY ]
0real trades
ever placed
Every result on this page (the +79.2%, the report card, the near-misses, all of it) comes from testing against historical data or "paper" (pretend-money) trading. The live trading system was fully built and even proven safe to restart mid-trade without duplicating an order. It was simply never turned on with real money, because it never earned the right to be. Every single event ever logged by this bot is the bot measuring itself. Not one of them is an actual trade.

In fairness, this page shouldn't overclaim the failure either: two of the five signals (company fundamentals and Reddit sentiment) were never properly tested at all, so they're unproven, not disproven. And the one genuinely strong, statistically significant result, that the backtest beat random chance by a wide margin, is still unexplained. The leading theory is residual noise, not a proven fact.
